EV & Fleet

EV Fleet Transition
Planning

A Colorado-based fleet operator managing around 30 mixed commercial vehicles needed a rigorous framework for evaluating an ICE-to-EV transition — one that accounted for vehicle selection, charging infrastructure at two facilities, grid capacity constraints, and available incentive funding. We built it.

Sector
Commercial Fleet Operations
Fleet Size
~30 vehicles (mixed classes)
Location
Colorado (two facilities)
Incentive Program
Colorado EVIE

The Situation

The client's executive team had made a commitment to electrify the fleet by a specific year — partly for sustainability goals, partly in response to Colorado's emissions policy direction, and partly because fuel and maintenance costs on their aging ICE vehicles were climbing. But the commitment had been made without a detailed plan for how to actually execute it.

Their fleet included light-duty passenger vehicles, mid-size vans, and a handful of heavier-duty utility vehicles — each with different daily range requirements, payload needs, and replacement timelines. They had two facilities with different electrical infrastructure. And nobody on staff had evaluated an EV fleet transition before. They needed a structured framework, not more research to do on their own.

The Challenge

  • No evaluation framework to compare EV models across different vehicle classes and duty cycles
  • Two facilities with different electrical panel capacity — one near its usable limit
  • Uncertainty about whether grid upgrades were required and what they would cost
  • Multiple overlapping incentive programs (federal, state, utility-level) with different eligibility rules and deadlines
  • Pressure to move fast, but no phasing strategy to manage capital expenditure
  • No internal expertise to evaluate charging hardware vendors or Level 2 vs DC fast charging tradeoffs

The Approach

Fleet electrification is an infrastructure project as much as a vehicle procurement project. We approached it in parallel workstreams so the vehicle and charging decisions could inform each other rather than being made in sequence.

  • Fleet duty cycle analysis: Documented the daily mileage, payload requirements, and route profiles for each vehicle category. Identified that three vehicle classes were clear EV candidates with available models (range well within daily duty cycle); one class had limited EV options and was deferred to Phase 2 pending market availability.
  • Vehicle evaluation matrix: Built a weighted scoring matrix across range, payload rating, total cost of ownership over five years (purchase + fuel + maintenance delta vs ICE), availability, and serviceability in Colorado. Evaluated six models across three manufacturers. Produced a ranked recommendation with supporting data the client team could validate and own.
  • Charging infrastructure modeling: For each facility, calculated the number of Level 2 chargers required based on fleet size, overnight dwell time, and charge rates. Modeled load additions to the existing electrical service. Facility 1 could support Phase 1 vehicles with a subpanel addition; Facility 2 required either load management software or a service upgrade — we modeled both options with cost and timeline estimates.
  • Utility engagement: Initiated an interconnection inquiry with the serving utility for Facility 2 to get an official grid capacity assessment and estimated upgrade costs. This turned out to be a significant project-risk item — the utility timeline for a service upgrade was 9–14 months, which had to be reflected in the phasing plan.
  • Incentive mapping: Documented federal tax credit eligibility under IRA Section 45W (commercial clean vehicles), Colorado EVIE grant eligibility for both vehicles and charging infrastructure, and the client's serving utility's EV fleet rate program. Produced a prioritized application calendar with deadlines and required documentation.
  • Phased rollout plan: Phase 1 targeted the 10 light-duty passenger vehicles — lowest capital cost, best EV model availability, and routes with the most predictable mileage. Phase 2 covered the vans after Facility 2's charging infrastructure was resolved. Phase 3 deferred the specialty utility vehicles pending market availability.

Outcomes

EVIE
Fleet qualified for Colorado EVIE incentive funding — vehicles and charging infrastructure
Phase 1
10 light-duty vehicles converted in Phase 1 pilot — on budget, on schedule
Deferred
Facility 2 grid upgrade deferred via load management software — significant capital cost avoided in Phase 1
Reusable
Decision framework and evaluation matrix owned by the client team — ready to apply as fleet grows

The utility timeline finding was the most impactful piece of the engagement for the client. They had been assuming Facility 2's infrastructure work would take three months; the actual utility estimate came back at 9–14 months. Without that in the plan, Phase 2 would have been delayed by nearly a year with no warning. Building that discovery into the phasing — and identifying the load management software as a Phase 1 bridge — kept the overall project on track.
